Two rules explain where every bit of push and flow in a circuit goes. Gustav Kirchhoff worked out both of them in 1845, while he was still a student. Bear that in mind the next time you can't find your lighter.

# The Junction Rule
Whatever flows into a junction flows out of it. Nothing piles up there, and nothing goes missing. If 100 Ig comes down a Wick and splits three ways, the three branches add up to exactly 100 Ig. It's why four machines on one Hookah get 25 Ig each, with none left over. Its real name is **Kirchhoff's current law**.

# The Loop Rule
Go once around any closed loop, adding up the push you gain at sources and the push you spend in parts, and you come back to exactly zero. Every Kerberos a Hookah gives, the loop spends before it gets home: put two equal Resinators in a loop on a 4.2 Kb Hookah and each one spends 2.1 Kb. Its real name is **Kirchhoff's voltage law**.

# Series: One After Another
Parts in series carry the same flow, and their squeezes add up: 1,000 Φ and 2,200 Φ in a row make **3,200 Φ**. A Daisy Wick wires blocks this way.

# Parallel: Side by Side
Parts in parallel get the same push, and the flow splits between them, more of it down the easier path. Together they squeeze *less* than either one alone:

**1 ÷ total = 1 ÷ first + 1 ÷ second**

Two 1,000 Φ Resinators side by side make **500 Φ**. Every path you add makes it easier for the flow to get through, which is why each machine you add to a Hookah's line draws more from it. A Twin Wick wires blocks this way.

# The Voltage Divider
Two Resinators in series between the rails split the push between them, in proportion to their squeeze:

**push across the lower = rail push × lower ÷ (upper + lower)**

With 1,000 Φ above and 2,200 Φ below on a 4.2 Kb rail: 4.2 × 2,200 ÷ 3,200 = **2.9 Kb**. Read the lower Resinator and see. This is the trick inside every volume knob ever made.
